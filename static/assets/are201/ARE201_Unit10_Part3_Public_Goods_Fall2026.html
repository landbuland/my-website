Would anyone build this for profit?

Vote first

Cape Hatteras Lighthouse has warned ships off the Outer Banks since 1870. Every ship that passes benefits. Could a company build one and make a profit?

Hands up first. Then click A or B to see one way it could go.
One way it could go: your company builds the tower and sends every passing ship a bill. Why pay for a light you can see for free? Few captains do. The fees don’t cover the cost, and the light goes dark.
One way it could go: no company builds it, so the government does. That is what happened here: Congress set aside $75,000 in 1867, and the U.S. Lighthouse Board finished the tower in 1870 for about $167,500 of tax money.

two questions

Two questions sort every good

1 · Rival?

“If I use it, is there less for you?”

Rival: a cheeseburger, a seat on a full bus.
Nonrival: a song you stream, a radio broadcast.

2 · Excludable?

“Can you stop people who don’t pay?”

Excludable: jeans, a concert behind a gate.
Nonexcludable: national defense, a hurricane warning.

Trap: rivalry can change with crowding (an empty road vs. a jammed one). Excludability does not: it depends only on whether you can put up a gate, by technology or by law.
Definitions: OpenStax Sec. 13.3 (nonrivalrous, nonexcludable); Krugman and Wells, Microeconomics 5e, Ch. 17.

Free riders: a prisoner’s dilemma

A free rider enjoys a good without paying for it. When a good is nonexcludable, everyone is tempted to free ride, so the market provides too little or none.

1

2
Both keep: $0 eachBoth giving would earn $6 each: a prisoner’s dilemma (Unit 9).
3
Like a positive externalityThe good is worth more to society ($16) than to the one who pays for it ($8).

so who pays?

How public goods get paid for

Taxes and government

Everyone must pay, so no one can free ride. Defense, police, hurricane warnings.

Advertising

Free podcasts and radio: you pay with your attention.

Social pressure

People give more when others can see: neighborhood watches, alumni giving, a church roof fund.

Make it excludable

Subscriptions and gates turn it into a club good.

Water and sewer: one network serves a town most cheaply (Unit 9’s natural monopoly), so towns usually build and run them with tax or utility money.
The lighthouse twist: in England from the 1600s to the 1800s, some lighthouses had private owners. The Crown gave each owner the right to charge ships a set fee, paid at the port. Private owners, but the government built the gate.
Lighthouses: R. H. Coase, “The Lighthouse in Economics,” Journal of Law and Economics 17(2), 1974; on the government’s role, E. Bertrand, Cambridge Journal of Economics 30(3), 2006. Ways to pay: OpenStax Sec. 13.3.

common resources

The tragedy of the commons

Southern flounder (no numbers)
1
Rival, and hard to excludeAny licensed angler can fish, and licenses are cheap. Each flounder you catch is one fewer for everyone else.
2
Each fisher counts only their own costNot the cost to other fishers of a smaller catch tomorrow: a negative externality.
3
So: overuseQMKT > QOPT. Garrett Hardin (1968) called it the tragedy of the commons.
If one more unit costs you nothing, you use it until your marginal benefit hits zero: the same reason plants pollute until MSB = 0 (Wednesday). North Carolina has cut the southern flounder catch sharply and shortened the season to a few weeks.
Hardin, “The Tragedy of the Commons,” Science 162, 1968. Flounder: NC Division of Marine Fisheries, Southern Flounder Fishery Management Plan, Amendment 3 (2022). Graph after Krugman and Wells, Fig. 17-4.
common resources

Saving the commons

Licenses and limits

Cap how many can fish, and how much each may take.

Seasons

Close the fishery while the fish spawn.

A price on each unit

London’s congestion charge (2003): pay to drive downtown at rush hour.

Property rights

Give someone the right, and a reason to protect it.

Tradable quotas

Cap the total catch, then let fishers trade shares (Wednesday’s permits).

Community rules

Users who know each other set and enforce their own rules (Elinor Ostrom, Nobel Prize 2009).

“Chickens are owned and plentiful; wild fish are unowned and scarce.” Each tool puts up a gate, so the commons becomes partly excludable.
Tools: OpenStax Sec. 13.3; Krugman and Wells, Microeconomics 5e lecture slides, Ch. 17. Ostrom: Nobel Prize lecture (2009).

in-class work · on paper · question 3 of 5

Holiday lights on Hillsborough Street

Choose one

A holiday light display on Hillsborough Street would cost $6,000. Each of the street’s 20 shops would gain $500 in extra sales. The shops are asked to chip in voluntarily. Why might the lights never go up, even though they are worth $10,000 in all?

AThe display costs more than it is worth
BThe lights are nonrival, so no shop wants them
CEach shop hopes the others will pay: the free-rider problem
DThe lights create a negative externality
C. Split evenly, each shop would pay $300 for $500 of benefit, but no shop can be kept from enjoying the lights, so each would rather let the others pay. A merchants’ association that charges every shop a fee fixes it.

in-class work · on paper · question 4 of 5

Wild ginseng

Choose one

On public mountain land, anyone can dig wild ginseng, and the roots sell for a high price. Plants are getting hard to find. Which policy most directly prevents overharvesting?

APay diggers a subsidy for each root
BA price ceiling on ginseng
CA digging season and a limited number of permits
DAdvertise ginseng’s health benefits
C. Wild ginseng on open land is a common resource (rival, nonexcludable). A season and a cap on permits put up a gate. A subsidy or more demand means more digging; a price ceiling does not limit the harvest.

Excludable?A college education is nonexcludable.

FalseA college can turn people away: no admission and tuition, no seat. Even a public university is excludable (page 4).

TrueAnyone nearby can look up, and there is no way to charge them. It is nonrival too: a public good.

exercise 4 of 4 · public goods and externalities · hands up

The same problem as a positive externality?

Vote first

True or false: the problem with public goods is like the problem with positive externalities, because the marginal social benefit is greater than any one person’s marginal benefit.

ATrue✓ Answer On page 7, chipping in is worth $16 to society but only $8 to Ray, who pays $10. Alone, he keeps his money.
BFalseNo Each person counts only their own benefit. What everyone else gains is left out, just as with a positive externality.
A public good’s MSB adds up everyone’s benefit, far above what any one person would pay. So markets underprovide both, and both call for public money: a tax-funded good or a subsidy.
