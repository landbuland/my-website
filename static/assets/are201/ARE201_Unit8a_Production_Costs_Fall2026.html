Production: a farm turns inputs into output

Production is the process of combining inputs to produce outputs—ideally output worth more than the inputs. A farm is a firm: it decides what to grow, how, how much, and how many people to hire. You just made that last decision.

The field, already planted with sweet potatoes

Technology

In the game only one input changed: the number of workers. The field, the digger and the truck stayed the same. A production function shows how much output a firm gets from its inputs—here, bushels per day for each crew size.

LO 8a.2 · name what you saw

Marginal product: what one more worker adds

Marginal product of labor (MP): the extra output from hiring one more worker, holding the other inputs fixed. Total product (TP) is total output: Pine Hollow’s bushels per day.
MP =change in total productchange in workers

Each step adds one worker, so the bottom of the fraction is 1: MP is simply the change in total product.

1
Maria, the 1st worker: 0 → 40 bushelsMP = 40 − 0 = 40
2
Jamal, the 2nd worker: 40 → 100 bushelsMP = 100 − 40 = 60. Jamal added more than Maria.

Maria drives, picks, boxes and hauls. While she carries a crate to the truck, the digger sits still.

Maria drives and Jamal picks right behind the digger. Nobody runs back and forth, so the digger never stops.

LO 8a.1 · the pattern has a name

Diminishing marginal returns: Unit 3 named it

Diminishing marginal returns occur when, with technology and other inputs held constant, successive units of one input eventually generate smaller additions to total output.
1
Hold everything else fixedSame land, same machines, same know-how. Only one input changes.
2
Add that input one unit at a timeOne more worker, one more acre, one more bag of fertilizer.
3
Each new unit eventually adds lessThe first few may even add more. But sooner or later the additions shrink. Total output still grows, just more slowly.
Why do the additions shrink? Two everyday pictures next: a farm that runs out of good land, and a checkout lane that runs out of room.

Unit 3 named the idea. Today we measure it, and at the end of class we put a price on it.

Example 1 · farming · no numbers needed

Expand onto poorer land

A farmer plants the best land first. Every new acre is a little drier, rockier or steeper than the last, so each one adds a smaller harvest.

Before each click: will this acre add as much as the last one? The silo keeps filling, but each band is thinner.

Here the later acres are worse land. Next: a case where every new worker is just as good as the last, and each still adds less.

Example 2 · crowding a fixed input · no numbers needed

One checkout lane, more and more baggers

Fixed: one register and one bagging counter. Variable: baggers. The first bagger frees the cashier to just scan. After that, every bagger has to share the same small counter.

Every bagger is just as good as the last. Each one adds less because the counter does not grow.

Pine Hollow is the same story: one digger, more and more workers.

LO 8a.1 · game debrief

Game debrief: where—and why—MP fell

Workers in hiring order: Maria 40 · Jamal 60 · Kenji 50 · Priya 30 · Luis 20 · Hannah 10 · Omar −10

1
Where did diminishing returns begin? Worker #3, Kenji: MP fell 60 → 50, the first MP smaller than the one before. On the graph, that is where TP starts to flatten.
2
Why did MP rise, then fall? Jamal let Maria specialize, so MP rose. After that everyone shared one digger, a fixed input—not lazier workers. Any hiring order gives the same numbers.
3
When MP fell, did total output fall? No. TP kept rising (150 → 210) while MP shrank. Only a negative MP—Omar’s −10—made TP fall, 210 → 200.
4
Does MP always rise first? No. Rise-then-fall is the typical shape; the law only says MP eventually falls—sometimes from the very first unit, like a bag of fertilizer on an already-rich field.
Why MP fell · one input was fixed · guess first

Fixed inputs vs. variable inputs

Fixed input

Your guess: what does it mean?

Cannot be changed quickly. The farm has the same amount whether it digs a lot or a little.

Variable input

Your guess: what does it mean?

Can be changed quickly. Dig more, and the farm uses more of it.

At Pine Hollow this harvest season, which inputs are fixed? Which are variable?
The digger and the leased field stay the same however many people dig; workers, diesel and boxes go up and down with the digging.

Each input brings a cost: fixed inputs bring fixed costs, variable inputs bring variable costs. We put dollars on them after the next few slides.

The time horizon

Short run vs. long run

The last slide’s two answers have names.

Short run

A period so short that at least one input is fixed.

Pine Hollow, this harvest season: the digger and the leased field are fixed. Only workers, diesel and boxes can change.

Long run

A period long enough that every input is variable.

Pine Hollow, five years out: lease a second digger (the game’s last screen), rent more acres, build a bigger curing room—or sell the farm.

The long run is not a date on the calendar. It is “long enough to change every input.” For a food truck that might be weeks; for an orchard, years.
Diminishing returns are a short-run story: they happen because something is fixed. Pine Hollow’s MP fell because the digger could not grow.

From inputs to costs · LO 8a.3 · guess first

Fixed costs vs. variable costs

Fixed cost (FC)

Your guess: what does it mean?

The cost of the fixed inputs. It stays the same however much the farm produces in the short run, and is paid even if nothing is produced.

Variable cost (VC)

Your guess: what does it mean?

The cost of the variable inputs. It rises when the farm produces more, and is zero when nothing is produced.

FC = $400 + $350 + $150 = $900 a day, even if nobody digs.

VC = $120 a day per worker (diesel and boxes folded in): 3 workers → $360; nobody → $0. Total cost: TC = FC + VC.

Illustrative numbers. The test for any cost: does it change if the farm digs one more bushel? Next: the daily cost table.

From totals to cost per bushel · guess first

Which day digs cheaper bushels?

Same farm, two days. Day B’s bill is bigger. Does that mean its bushels cost more? Vote first.

Split each day’s bill over its bushels: every crate carries its share.

Day B’s bill is bigger, but twice as many bushels share it, so each bushel carries less. That share is the average cost of a bushel.

The total tells you the size of the bill; the average tells you what each bushel carries, the number to hold up against the $8 a bushel the packing house pays.

LO 8a.3 · cost per bushel

Four ways to measure cost per unit

Average fixed cost

AFC = FC ÷ Q

The $900 spread over every bushel. Falls as Q rises.

Average variable cost

AVC = VC ÷ Q

Wages per bushel. Next unit (8b) uses AVC to decide when to shut down.

Average total cost

ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC

The full cost of a typical bushel.

Marginal cost

MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ

The cost of the next bushel only.

Divide by the change in output, not by 1. Going from 2 to 3 workers adds 50 bushels for $120: MC = $120 ÷ 50 = $2.40. That is MC = wage ÷ MP: one more worker costs the wage and adds MP bushels. Quiz tables that add one unit at a time make MC look like a plain difference in TC—because ΔQ = 1.

Reading: Lumen “Costs in the Short Run” and “Average Costs and Curves.” One correction: where it says the ATC of 80 haircuts is $8, the table gives $560 ÷ 80 = $7.

Cost per bushel, one at a time · 1 of 4

AFC: one bill, more and more bushels

The $900 bill is the same every day, however many people dig. AFC spreads it over all the bushels the crew digs.

The bill never changes, but more bushels share it, so AFC keeps falling: fast at first, then more and more slowly. It never turns up.

Cost per bushel, one at a time · 2 of 4

MC: what the next bushel costs

Hiring one more worker costs $120 and adds that worker’s pile. MC spreads the $120 over the new pile only.

A big new pile makes the next bushel cheap; a small one makes it dear. MC is lowest with Jamal and shoots up as the new piles shrink: the mirror image of MP.

Cost per bushel, one at a time · 3 of 4

AVC: why does it dip, then rise?

Every worker costs $120 a day. AVC spreads that $120 over the bushels the average worker digs: a bigger pile means a cheaper bushel.

The newest worker pulls the average toward their own number. Digs more than the average: the pile grows, AVC falls. Digs less: the pile shrinks, AVC rises. Diminishing returns start with Kenji, but AVC rises only from Priya, the first worker below the average.

Cost per bushel, one at a time · 4 of 4

ATC = AFC + AVC

ATC is the whole cost of a typical bushel: its share of the $900 bill (AFC, purple) plus its share of the wages (AVC, green).

At first the shrinking purple part wins, so ATC falls. Later the growing green part wins, so ATC turns up. The bottom, $7.50 at 200 bushels, is where the two pulls balance.

LO 8a.4 · the rule

Where MC crosses ATC, ATC is at its lowest

MC < ATC

ATC falls.

Pine Hollow, 180 → 200 bu: MC $6.00 < ATC $7.67, so ATC falls to $7.50.

MC = ATC

ATC is at its minimum.

MC crosses ATC from below near 200 bushels, where ATC bottoms out at $7.50.

MC > ATC

ATC rises.

200 → 210 bu: MC $12.00 > ATC $7.50, so ATC rises to $7.71.

The same holds for AVC, as the money-bag slide showed: a new worker who digs more than the average pulls AVC down, less pulls it up. Kenji digs exactly the average, so his MC of $2.40 (100 → 150 bu) equals the lowest AVC, $2.40.
Trap: “MC is rising, so ATC must be rising.” No—at Pine Hollow MC rises from $2 to $6 while ATC keeps falling, because MC is still below ATC. Compare the levels, not the direction.

LO 8a.4 · concept check

Quick check

“When marginal cost is above average total cost, average total cost must be falling.”

FalseMC above ATC pulls ATC up.

“A rise in fixed cost raises marginal cost.”

FalseMC counts only what changes with one more unit; FC does not.

A firm makes 10 units at ATC = $20. The 11th unit has MC = $9. Does ATC rise or fall, and to what?

Falls($200 + $9) ÷ 11 = $19.

“The gap between ATC and AVC is largest at small output.”

TrueThe gap is AFC, which shrinks as Q grows.

Why higher costs shift supply to the left

Unit 4 said

“Input prices: higher wages or materials costs → supply left.”

Also: drought shifts farm supply left; a per-unit fee raises marginal cost.

Today explains why

Higher input prices or lower MP push the MC curve up.

At any given price, the farm now finds fewer bushels worth digging.

Coming in 8b: in perfect competition, a firm’s supply curve is its MC curve—the part above AVC. That is why today we also learned AVC.
And why a rise in fixed cost (the lease, insurance) does not shift that MC curve at all.
Optional · a peek at the long run

In the long run, every input can change

Economies of scale

When a firm grows all its inputs together, its long-run average cost can fall. A large hog farm spreads a Prop 12 renovation over many more pigs than a small one.

Not the same as today’s curves

Today’s rising MC is a short-run story: one digger, more workers. Economies of scale are a long-run story: everything grows together.

Trap: “Demand for cage-free eggs jumps, so in the short run marginal cost falls because of economies of scale.” No—with barns fixed, producing more raises MC.

We return to economies of scale when we study market structure.
