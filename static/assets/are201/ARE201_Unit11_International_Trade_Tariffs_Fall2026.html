from raleigh to canada

Domestic trade and international trade

Domestic trade

Buying and selling goods or services within the same country.

A construction company in Raleigh buys lumber from a sawmill in North Carolina.

Opening to international trade

Letting buyers and sellers in different countries trade with one another.

The same Raleigh company buys lumber from a Canadian sawmill.

Same market, more sellers. What happens to the price, and who gains? We follow the US lumber market for the rest of the week.
key vocabulary

Three prices in a model of trade

Domestic equilibrium

The price and quantity if the market were closed to trade.

Also called the autarky price (autarky = no trade).

World price (PW)

The price foreign suppliers charge. We assume imported and domestic products are identical.

We also assume the world price does not change when we buy more or less.

Tariff (t)

A tax on imported goods or services. The domestic price becomes PW + t.

Quiz graphs write the price with the tariff as PT, so the tariff is PT − PW.

A tariff applies to imports, not directly to each unit produced at home.

a long-running case: canadian lumber sold in the united states

Why would a country impose a tariff?

Pressure to protect domestic firms

When cheaper imports compete with local producers, domestic firms may lose sales and workers may face disruption.

The policy trade-off

A tariff can help domestic producers compete, but it also makes imported goods more expensive for domestic buyers.

Think

Would a lumber tariff help all Americans equally?

No. US sawmills and their workers gain. Home builders, and the families who buy new homes, pay more. The rest of today measures each side.

Why do imports fall after a tariff?

Supply response

US producers can sell more at a higher price.

Output: 10m → 30m

Demand response

US consumers buy less at a higher price.

Purchases: 70m → 50m

Imports shrink on both margins: 60 million → 20 million (20 fewer because mills make more, 20 fewer because buyers buy less).
the tax changes incentives, even though local output is not taxed

A tariff is a tax on imports

Imported lumber

Importers pay the tariff to the government. The domestic market price rises to the world price plus the tariff.

Domestic lumber

US mills do not pay the import tariff on their own output, but they can sell at the higher market price.

Model assumptions: imported and domestic lumber are perfect substitutes; the world price stays fixed at $400.

Who writes the check is not the same as who bears the cost. Coming up: who really pays a tariff?

two triangles, two kinds of waste

Why is there a deadweight loss?

B: production inefficiency

Output rises from 10m to 30m, though these units cost US mills more than the $400 import price. Making them at home uses more resources than importing them. B = ½ × 20m × $400 = $4 million.

D: consumption inefficiency

Purchases fall from 70m to 50m. Buyers valued these units above $400 but below $800. Purchases worth making no longer happen. D = ½ × 20m × $400 = $4 million.

DWL = B + D = $8 million: the tariff shrinks the gains from trade. The course slides call B and D the production and consumption distortions.

who gains, who loses: one group at a time

The tariff’s four effects

Lumber with a $400 tariff

Effect 1: consumers lose

Price $400 → $800; purchases 70m → 50m.
Loss = A + B + C + D = $8m + $4m + $8m + $4m = $24 million.

Consumers lose all four shaded areas.

Effect 2: domestic producers gain

Price $400 → $800; output 10m → 30m.
Gain = A = ½ × (10 + 30) × $400 = $8 million.

The producers’ gain is a transfer from domestic consumers.

Effect 3: the government collects revenue

Government revenue = t × imports = $400 × 20m.
Government revenue = C = $8 million.

Only the imports that remain are taxed: the tariff wedge times the post-tariff imports.

Effect 4: deadweight loss

B = ½ × 20m × $400 = $4m; D = $4m.
DWL = B + D = $8 million.

The producers’ gain and the government revenue do not fully offset the consumers’ loss.

What a tariff does

Price and quantities

Price rises to PW + t. Domestic output rises, consumption falls, imports fall on both margins.

Transfers

Consumers lose A + B + C + D. Producers gain A. The government gets C = t × imports.

Deadweight loss

B (too much made at home) + D (too little bought). Total surplus falls.

Tariffs in Practice

Why countries use them, who really pays, and the Canadian lumber case
trade policy can favor some groups even when total surplus falls

Why use tariffs if they cause a deadweight loss?

Reasons for support

• Protect a domestic industry
• Support domestic workers
• Respond to political pressure from producers
• Answer “dumping”: foreign firms selling below their cost

The economic trade-off

• Domestic sellers gain
• Domestic buyers lose
• The government gains revenue
• Total domestic surplus falls

Hands up

The lumber tariff costs buyers $24 million and gives mills $8 million. Why might Congress still pass it?

The gain is concentrated: a few mills, each with a lot at stake, lobby hard. The loss is spread out over millions of buyers, each losing a little. Few of them call Congress.
The textbook counts domestic surplus only; foreign producers’ surplus is outside its accounting. Reasons from the course slides and OpenStax, Principles of Economics (1st ed.), Ch. 19, Key Concepts 19.4, which also notes that countries negotiate lower barriers through the World Trade Organization (WTO).

2
Who bears the cost?In our model the world price stays at $400, so the US price rises by the full $400. US buyers pay all of it.
3
Same idea as Unit 6The side that cannot get away bears the tax. Foreign supply is flat (perfectly elastic) at PW, so foreign mills give up nothing.
4
What happened in 2018?Studies of the 2018 US tariffs found that prices of the taxed imports rose by about the full tariff: foreign sellers did not cut their prices.
Foreigners pay part of a tariff only if it pushes the world price down, which takes a very large buyer and foreign sellers with nowhere else to sell.

key takeaways · keep these visible in every graph

Unit 11 in three relationships

Free trade

PW below the domestic price: imports = Qd − Qs. PW above it: exports = Qs − Qd.

Tariff

The domestic price rises; local production expands, consumption contracts, and imports fall.

Welfare

Consumers lose A + B + C + D; producers gain A; government gains C; DWL = B + D.

A tariff changes who gains from trade and reduces total surplus.
