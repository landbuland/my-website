What is an externality?

An externality (or spillover) is a cost or benefit that falls on a third party: someone who is neither the buyer nor the seller.

private vs. social

The market sees only part of the picture

Costs

MSC = MPC + MEC
MPCmarginal private cost: what the seller pays for one more unit (feed, labor). The supply curve. MECmarginal external cost: what one more unit costs the third party. MSCmarginal social cost: the cost to everyone.

Benefits

MSB = MPB + MEB
MPBmarginal private benefit: what the buyer would pay for one more unit. The demand curve. MEBmarginal external benefit: what one more unit is worth to the third party. MSBmarginal social benefit: the value to everyone.
The market only sees the private curves (supply and demand). Society counts both.
A firm that does not pay its external cost does not face its full cost. Make it pay, and its marginal cost rises.
Unit 5: benefits are measured by willingness to pay for one more unit. MSB adds what everyone else would pay.

negative externality · the cost of too much

Why 50,000 hogs is too many

Hogs (made-up numbers)
1
Take the 45,000th hogBuyers value it at $170 (MSB). It costs society $190 (MSC): −$20.
2
Every hog from 40,000 to 50,000costs society more than it is worth. At 50,000 the gap is the full $40 external cost.
3
Deadweight loss½ × 10 thousand × $40 = $200,000 a week, lost every week.
Unit 5 called this overproduction: units whose cost is above their value. The lost value is a deadweight loss.

the fix

A $40 tax: the same wedge as Unit 6

Hogs with a $40 tax per hog (made-up numbers)

Unit 6: a $1 gas tax

No externality in the model, so the wedge created a deadweight loss.

Today: a $40 hog tax

The tax equals the external cost, so the wedge removes the deadweight loss: 40,000 hogs.

Buyers pay $180 (+$20); farms keep $140 (−$20).

A tax equal to the marginal external cost is a Pigouvian tax (after Arthur Pigou). Now the farm internalizes the externality: it counts the neighbors’ cost as its own.

Price rises $20, not $40: buyers and farms split the tax, as in Unit 6 (here the two curves are equally steep).

positive externality · step by step

Flu shots: the opposite problem

Flu shots near campus (made-up numbers)
0
The market: MPB meets MPCStudents count their own benefit only: 25,000 shots at $35.
1
Add the benefit to others: $20 a shotMSB = MPB + MEB: fewer roommates and classmates get sick.
2
What society wants: MSB = MSC35,000 shots. The market makes 10,000 too few.
3
Deadweight loss½ × 10 thousand × $20 = $100,000: shots worth more than they cost, never given.
4
Fix: a $20 subsidy per shotThe clinic gets $45; students pay $25. Now 35,000 shots.

The subsidy equals the marginal external benefit (MSB − MPB), not the whole MSB. Pay it to buyers (a voucher) or to sellers (per shot given): same 35,000 shots, as with Unit 6’s tax.

Made-up numbers: MPB: P = $60 − Q; MEB = $20; MPC = MSC: P = $10 + Q (no spillover from making shots); Q in thousands. OpenStax Fig. 13.3 uses the same story.

One rule: the socially optimal quantity is where MSB = MSC. Find the market’s quantity, find society’s, and the gap between them is the deadweight loss.
A good the government subsidizes probably has a positive externality; a good it taxes for this reason, a negative one.

quiz and exam essays

How to write about an externality

___ is a [negative / positive] externality because [third party] [bear a cost / get a benefit] that [decision maker] [doesn’t pay for / isn’t paid for]. So MSC > MPC or MSB > MPB, and the market produces [more / less] than the socially optimal quantity, where MSB = MSC.
Filled in
Hog farming is a negative externality because neighbors of the farm bear a cost that the farm doesn’t pay for. So MSC > MPC, and the market produces more than the socially optimal quantity, where MSB = MSC.
Graders look for three things: who the third party is, which curve is higher (MSC vs. MPC or MSB vs. MPB), and too much or too little.

Cleaner rivers, too few filters

Vote first

A coastal company makes water filters. Each one it sells also cleans the nearby rivers, but it makes fewer than is best for the community. Which policy would best raise production to the socially optimal level?

AA city-wide campaign about the value of clean waterNo It may nudge demand a little, but it does not reward the spillover on each unit.
BA one-time grant to expand the factoryNo A lump sum does not change the reward for each extra filter, so it is a weak lever on how many get made.
CA rule that every local company must install one, whatever the costNo A blunt command: it ignores whether each installation is worth its cost.
DA per-unit subsidy for each filter produced✓ Answer Each extra filter now earns its external benefit, so the company makes more: a Pigouvian subsidy.
EA per-unit tax on each filter producedNo A tax raises the cost of each unit and cuts production: the wrong direction.
Positive externality → per-unit subsidy = MEB. Negative externality → per-unit tax = MEC.
