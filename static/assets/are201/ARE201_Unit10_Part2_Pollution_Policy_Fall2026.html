The cheap fix comes first, so each extra ton costs more to cut: $20, then $60, then $100. Cutting all 60 tons ends the protest, but the last 20 cost $100 a ton. Is every cut worth it? Next: the graph.

No rules vs. the right amount

Tons emitted (made-up numbers)
1
No rules: 60 tonsMonday’s market rule: MPB = MPC. Here MPC of pollution = $0, so farms emit every ton that saves them anything, until MSB = 0. They ignore the MSC.
2
Best amount: 40 tonsWhere MSB = MSC = $40. Not zero: each of the first 40 tons saves more than it harms.
3
Past 40 tons: MSC > MSBAt 50 tons the next ton harms $50 and saves only $20. All the tons from 40 to 60 lose ½ × 20 × $60 = $600 a week.
Socially optimal pollution: MSB of pollution = MSC of pollution. Below it, MSB > MSC; above it, MSC > MSB. With no rules, farms go all the way to MSB = 0.

Getting to 40 tons: a tax or a cap

Tons emitted, with a tax per ton (made-up numbers)
1
A Pigouvian tax of $40 a tonThe tax lifts the farms’ MPC of pollution from $0 to $40, so they stop where MSB = $40: 40 tons (the filter pays, injecting does not). Size: the MSC at the optimum.
2
Too low: a $20 taxFarms stop where MSB = $20: 50 tons, too much pollution. There, MSC ($50) > MSB ($20).
3
Too high: a $60 tax30 tons: too little pollution. Cutting those last 10 tons cost more than they were worth.
4
Or a cap: 40 tradable permitsFarms bid for the 40 permits until a permit costs $40, the MSB at 40 tons. Same result.
Check the direction: a tax set too low leaves too much pollution, and at that amount MSC > MSB.

who should cut? · three rules · play the scene

Three rules for cutting 4 tons

Same plants, same costs. The state does not know the costs, but it picks the rule. Who cuts under each rule, and what does it cost?

Same 4 tons, $30 less. A price on each ton (a tax, or a permit that can be sold) lets the plant that can cut cheaply do most of the cutting, and no one has to tell the state the costs.
Made-up numbers. The $45 tax also collects $45 × 4 tons still emitted = $180: a transfer, not a cost to society (Unit 6). Any permit price from $30 to $60 works; at $45, each plant pays $15 less than under the standard.
option 4 · property rights · play the scene

Who holds the right: the farm or the neighbor?

One hog farm, one neighbor, no government rule. The two can talk. Does it matter who holds the right?

Coase theorem: if transaction costs are low, people can bargain to the efficient outcome, whoever holds the right. The right decides who pays, not what happens. The bargain internalizes the externality.

Paying the people who bear the cost (compensation) also makes the cost count. It raises a fairness question: who should pay when a few bear the cost and many enjoy the benefit?

Made-up numbers. Ronald Coase (Nobel Prize, 1991) used a railroad whose sparks set a farmer’s field on fire (OpenStax Sec. 12.3).
transaction costs · play the scene

When does bargaining work?

Left: Monday’s bees and the blueberries next door. Right: the same $30,000 cover, but the smell now reaches 200 households.

Transaction costs: all the costs of making a deal: finding everyone, agreeing on who pays, writing it down, enforcing it. Two parties: low. Hundreds: high, and each would rather let the others pay (Friday: free riders).
North Carolina: in 2018, federal juries ordered a Smithfield subsidiary to pay neighbors of large hog farms in eastern NC; the legislature then limited such nuisance suits (Farm Act of 2018). When bargaining fails, courts and legislatures decide who holds the right.
Bees: S. N. S. Cheung, “The Fable of the Bees,” J. Law & Econ. 16(1), 1973. NC: nuisance verdicts against Murphy-Brown LLC (E.D.N.C., 2018); NC Farm Act of 2018 (S.B. 711). Household numbers are made up.

Why no deal?

Vote first

A food truck parks every evening on a street with 40 families. Its generator is loud. In principle, the families could pay the owner to park somewhere else. Why doesn’t that happen?

AThe families don’t really mind the noise.No They mind; that is the externality.
BThe owner would turn down any offer.No An offer bigger than what the spot is worth to the owner makes the owner better off.
CGetting 40 families to agree on who pays how much takes too much time and effort.✓ Answer High transaction costs: finding everyone, agreeing on shares, stopping free riders.

Why economists criticize standards (OpenStax 12.2)

1 · No reward for beating the standard.   2 · One rule for everyone, cheap or costly to meet.   3 · Written in politics: loopholes and exceptions.

Real case: the 1990 Clean Air Act’s cap and trade for sulfur dioxide. Power-plant SO2 fell 36% from 1990 to 2004 while coal-fired power rose 25%, at a cost at least 15% (maybe up to 90%) below command-and-control.
SO2: R. Schmalensee and R. Stavins, “The SO2 Allowance Trading System,” Journal of Economic Perspectives 27(1), 2013. “Cap and trade” means tradable permits only.

Which tool is this?

For each one, pick: environmental standard, emissions tax, tradable emissions permits, or the Coase theorem.

1. The state requires every coal plant to install a scrubber on its smokestack.

Environmental standardA rule about equipment, the same for every plant.

2. The city charges each factory $25 for every ton of smoke it releases.

Emissions taxA price on each ton; each factory decides how much to cut.

3. The state caps a region at 1,000 tons a year and hands out permits that plants can buy and sell.

Tradable emissions permitsA cap on the total; trading decides who cuts.

4. A farmer pays the rancher upstream to fence cattle out of the creek they share.

Coase theorem (private bargaining)Two parties, low transaction costs: they strike the deal themselves.
Which fits where: millions of small sources → a tax; a few big sources → permits; two parties → bargaining; one must-do fix → a standard.

Wednesday · takeaways

Three things to take away

1
The right amount of pollution: MSB = MSC of pollutionUsually not zero. With no rules, polluters keep going until MSB = 0, where MSC > MSB.
2
A Pigouvian tax = the MSC at the optimal amountToo low a tax: too much pollution. Too high: too little. A cap with tradable permits gets the same result.
3
Let the cheap cutters cutTaxes and permits cut the same tons for less than a standard. Bargaining (Coase) works only when transaction costs are low.

What is the extra profit called?

Vote first

A factory could produce more, which would mean more pollution. The extra profit it would gain for each extra unit of pollution is the ______ of increasing pollution.

Amarginal benefit✓ Answer Each extra unit lets the factory skip a cleanup or make more: the MSB of pollution (page 3, where MPB = MSB).
Bmarginal private costNo With no rules, the factory pays $0 for each unit it emits: that is the MPC of pollution.
Coptimal level of pollutionNo That is an amount (40 tons on page 4), not a gain on each unit.
Dmarginal social costNo That is the harm the extra unit does to everyone else.
Polluting is tempting because each unit saves the polluter money: that saving is the marginal benefit (MSB) of pollution. The harm it does to others is the MSC.

No rules: too much or too little?

Vote first

When pollution is a negative externality and nothing makes the polluter pay, will there be too much pollution or too little?

AToo much pollution✓ Answer Polluters ignore the MSC and keep going until MSB = 0: 60 tons, not 40 (page 4).
BToo little pollutionNo Nothing holds them back: the harm falls on others.
CJust the right amountNo Only if polluters counted the MSC, and with no rules they do not.
No rules → polluters stop at MSB = 0. Past the optimum, MSC > MSB: each extra ton harms more than it saves.

Make the polluter pay

Vote first

A company is now forced to pay the external cost of its activity (for example, through a Pigouvian tax). What happens to its marginal cost?

AIts marginal cost rises.✓ Answer Each unit now carries the harm it does. On page 5, the $40 tax lifts the farms’ MPC of pollution from $0 to $40.
