From the goal to a rule · LO 8b.4

Should the orchard pick one more basket?

The cider mill pays the same for every basket: that is MR. Pickers are paid by the hour, so a basket’s cost (MC) depends on how long it takes to pick. Pick the first basket?

Why MC rises: you always pick the easiest apples first, so each extra basket takes longer.

One more basket changes profit by MR − MC. Keep picking while MR > MC; stop before MR < MC.
The rule · LO 8b.4

The most profit is where MR = MC

MR > MC

The next unit adds more than it costs: profit is still rising. Produce more.

MR = MC

The next unit adds nothing: the top of the hill. Stay here.

MR < MC

The last unit cost more than it brought in: profit is falling. Produce less.

Every firm, in every market, follows this rule. What the market decides is the firm’s MR.

MR = MC does not mean zero profit: every basket before the last one added profit (the green area). So what decides a firm’s MR? Its market. A story first.

Back to the sardine coast, after phones · LO 8b.2 · hands up

You caught 10 sardines. What price do you ask?

Going price: $5 a sardine; buyers can walk to any boat. Like every firm, you want the most profit.
To make the most profit, you take the price: you are a price taker. One boat is too small to move the coast’s price; the only thing you choose is how many fish to catch and sell. Caught 20 instead of 10? Still $5 each: every extra sardine brings in another $5.
LO 8b.2 · two different demand curves

One market, two demand curves

The coast’s market: slopes down

If sardines cost more everywhere, families buy fewer. Supply and demand along the whole coast set the $5.

Your boat: perfectly elastic

Unit 6: “Perfectly elastic: one penny more and you sell nothing.” At $5 you can sell 10 sardines or 20. Price Elasticity of Demand = ∞.

Trap: “In perfect competition, demand is perfectly elastic.” Only the firm’s demand. The market demand curve still slopes down.

LO 8b.2 & 8b.4 · new situations · true or false?

You be the judge

WheatWheat sells for $6 a bushel. Harvesting one more acre brings in 50 bushels and costs $250 in fuel and labor. The farmer should harvest that acre.

TrueMR = 50 × $6 = $300, more than MC of $250. Harvesting adds $50 to profit.

DairyA dairy’s 101st cow would bring in $12 a day in milk and cost $15 a day in feed and labor. More milk means more profit, so the dairy should add the cow.

FalseMR $12 < MC $15: profit falls $3 a day. More output is not the goal; more profit is.

CottonCotton is selling for 70¢ a pound. A grower with a big crop of the same grade can earn more by asking 75¢.

FalseBuyers can get identical cotton for 70¢. A price taker sells nothing above the going price.

Taco truckA taco truck sets its own prices, so it is not a price taker. That means the MR = MC rule doesn’t apply to it.

FalseEvery firm that wants the most profit uses MR = MC. What changes from firm to firm is MR.

HoneyA beekeeper’s profit per jar is highest at 300 jars, but at 300 jars MR is still above MC. The beekeeper should make more than 300 jars.

TrueThe goal is total profit. While MR > MC, each extra jar still adds profit, even as profit per jar falls.

LO 8b.6 · reading economic profit

Zero economic profit is not failure

Economic profit > 0

You beat your next-best option. Others will want in.

Economic profit = 0

You earn exactly what your next-best option pays: a normal profit.

Economic profit < 0

Your next-best option pays more. In time, you leave.

Economic profit ≤ accounting profit. They are equal only when implicit costs are zero.

LO 8b.6 · the long run

Short run vs. long run

The key difference is what the firm can adjust.

Short run

  • At least one input is fixed.
  • Fixed costs remain even if Q = 0.
  • The firm may keep operating with a loss if P > AVC.

Decision: produce, or temporarily shut down.

Taco truck: close for a rainy August, but the rent is still owed.

Long run

  • All inputs can be adjusted.
  • Fixed commitments, like a lease, can expire.
  • A firm does not stay forever in an industry with negative economic profit.

Decision: stay, enter, or exit.

Taco truck: don’t renew the truck rental; sell tacos no more.

Shutdown is a short-run production decision. Exit is a long-run industry decision.
Monday’s fourth ingredient, free entry and exit (page 7), is what lets firms come and go in the long run. In the long run, firms compare P with ATC: is there economic profit?
LO 8b.6 · long run · entry

Economic profit triggers entry

Profits attract new firms and expand industry supply.

1
Economic profitAt $17, P > ATC: each firm earns economic profit. Outsiders want in.
2
New firms enterMore sellers: market supply shifts right (Unit 4: number of sellers is a supply shifter). Price falls to $14.50; profit shrinks.
3
Entry stops at min ATCPrice falls to $12 = minimum ATC. Economic profit = 0: no reason left to enter.
Entry shifts market supply right → market price falls → each firm’s economic profit shrinks.
LO 8b.6 · long run · exit

Economic loss triggers exit

Persistent losses cause some firms to leave the industry.

1
Economic lossAt $8, P < ATC: each firm loses money. (P > AVC, so in the short run it keeps producing.)
2
Some firms exitWhen leases expire, some firms leave: market supply shifts left. Price rises to $10; losses shrink.
3
Exit stops at min ATCPrice rises to $12 = minimum ATC. Economic profit = 0: the firms that remain no longer want to leave.
Exit shifts market supply left → market price rises → losses shrink for the firms that remain.
