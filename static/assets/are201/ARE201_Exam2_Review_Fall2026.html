Unit 7 · total and marginal utility

MU shrinks; TU peaks where MU reaches zero

Priya drinks coffee during an all-day study session.

Cups12345
Total utility1424303027
Marginal utility141060−3

The rules

MU = change in TU from one more unit; it shrinks as you consume more.

MU > 0 → TU rises
MU = 0 → TU at its peak
MU < 0 → TU falls

1. What is the marginal utility of the 3rd cup?

MU = 30 − 24 = 6 utils.

2. How many cups give Priya the most total utility?

3 or 4 cups (TU = 30): the 4th cup adds nothing.

3. The 5th cup: what is its MU, and is total utility now negative?

MU = 27 − 30 = −3. TU falls but is still +27: negative MU lowers TU; it does not make TU negative.
Unit 7 · buy by marginal utility per dollar

Each dollar goes where it buys the most MU

Marco has $20 for lunches this week. Sandwiches cost $4; smoothies cost $2.

Units123456
TU, sandwiches32567280
MU ÷ P, sandwiches8642
TU, smoothies203648566062
MU ÷ P, smoothies1086421

1. Which item does Marco buy first? Why?

A smoothie: 20 ÷ $2 = 10 utils per dollar, versus 32 ÷ $4 = 8 for a sandwich.

2. Spend all $20 by MU per dollar. What bundle does he end up with?

3 sandwiches and 4 smoothies: $12 + $8 = $20. The last dollar on each buys 4 utils. TU = 72 + 56 = 128.

3. Check: is 4 sandwiches and 2 smoothies, also $20, any better?

No: 80 + 36 = 116 < 128. The 4th sandwich gives only 2 utils per dollar.
Unit 7 · the utility-maximizing rule

At the best bundle, MU ÷ P is equal

MUXPX = MUYPY and the whole budget is spent

1 · Fix a choice. Kayla has spent her whole budget. Her last dollar on yoga classes adds 6 utils; her last dollar on concert tickets adds 9. What should she change?

Move dollars from yoga to concerts. As she does, concerts' MU per dollar falls and yoga's rises, until the two are equal.

2 · Find a price. Sam is at his best bundle of apples and granola bars. The last apple adds 16 utils and costs $0.80; the last granola bar adds 30 utils. What does a granola bar cost?

Apples: 16 ÷ $0.80 = 20 utils per dollar. Granola must match: 30 ÷ P = 20, so P = $1.50.

3 · True or false? At the utility-maximizing bundle, the last unit of each good adds the same marginal utility.

False. What is equal is MU per dollar, MU ÷ P. The MUs themselves match only when the prices match.
Unit 7 · one price change, two effects

Two effects: which way, and which won?

Substitution effect

The good whose price rose is now relatively pricier → buy less of it and more of the other good.

Income effect

The same income buys less → buy less of every normal good (more of an inferior good).

For the other good the two effects push in opposite directions; what you observe tells you which one was bigger.

1. Electricity gets more expensive. Dana uses less electricity, and she also buys fewer paperback books. For books, which effect was larger?

The income effect. Substitution pushes toward more books (they are now relatively cheaper); the smaller budget pushes toward fewer. Books fell, so the income effect won.

2. Bus fares fall. Leo rides the bus more and takes fewer rideshare trips. Rideshares are a normal good for Leo. For rideshares, which effect was larger?

The substitution effect. Cheaper buses pull him away from rideshares; the extra buying power pushes toward more rideshares. Rideshares fell, so substitution won.
Unit 7 · move along or shift?

Along a demand curve, only own price changes

Lin buys oat-milk lattes. For each change: a movement along her demand curve, or a shift?

1. The price of an oat-milk latte falls from $6 to $5.

A movement down along Lin's demand curve: the own price changed.

2. Lin starts training for a marathon; every latte now feels more valuable to her (MU of each cup rises).

Demand shifts right: at every price she wants more. Tastes are held fixed along a demand curve.

3. Lin's income falls; lattes are a normal good for her.

Demand shifts left.

4. In one sentence: why does a demand curve slope down?

Each extra unit adds less MU, so a buyer pays less for it; and a higher price triggers the substitution and income effects.

Unit 6 · price elasticity of demand

Average bases, then compare |E| with 1

A campus craft booth raises the price of its handmade earrings from $25 to $35 a pair. Monthly sales fall from 20 pairs to 14. Demand did not shift. Find the price elasticity of demand.

Ed = [ΔQ ÷ average Q] ÷ [ΔP ÷ average P]

1

%ΔQ = −6 ÷ 17 = −35.3%  (average Q = 17)

2

%ΔP = +10 ÷ 30 = +33.3%  (average P = $30)

3

Ed = −35.3% ÷ 33.3% ≈ −1.06. |Ed| > 1 → elastic, just barely.

4

Check with revenue: $25 × 20 = $500 → $35 × 14 = $490. Price up, revenue down: consistent with elastic demand.

1. A spring frost wipes out part of the peach crop. The price of peaches jumps and growers' total revenue rises. What does that say about demand for peaches?

Demand is inelastic: price rose by a larger percentage than quantity fell, so P × Q went up.

2. Ana spends exactly $40 a month on streaming, whatever the subscription prices are. Her demand is…

Unit elastic: spending (P × Q) never changes, so every % change in price is matched by an equal % change in quantity.
Unit 6 · elasticity and total revenue · your turn

Classify demand, then predict revenue

For each move: elastic or inelastic? Did revenue rise or fall?

1 · Parking permits. A university raises the price of a parking permit by 10%; permits sold fall by 4%.

|Ed| ≈ 4 ÷ 10 = 0.4 → inelastic. Revenue rises (about 1.10 × 0.96 = 1.056, +5.6%).

2 · Matinee tickets. A movie theater cuts matinee prices by 20%; matinee attendance rises by 35%.

|Ed| ≈ 35 ÷ 20 = 1.75 → elastic. Revenue rises (0.80 × 1.35 = 1.08, +8%).

3. Both moves raised revenue, one with a price increase and one with a price cut. What is the general lesson?

Raise the price where demand is inelastic; cut it where demand is elastic. A seller who knows both elasticities can do both at once.
Unit 6 · what makes demand elastic

How easily can the buyer say no?

Substitutesmore → more elastic
Necessityluxury → more elastic
Budget sharebigger → more elastic
Timelonger → more elastic
Which statement is correct?
Choose an answer, then explain why.

Answer: C. A narrowly defined good, one brand, has many close substitutes (other brands), so its demand is more elastic than demand for a broad category. A is backwards (time), and B is backwards (smoke alarms are a necessity).

Unit 6 · the supply side

Es = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP, and it is always positive

1 · Compute. The price of blueberries falls 12%, and quantity supplied falls 6%. Find Es and classify it.

Es = (−6%) ÷ (−12%) = +0.5 → inelastic. Price and quantity supplied move together, so Es is positive whether they rise or fall.

2 · Time. A newly planted pecan orchard takes 6 to 10 years to bear nuts. Demand for pecans jumps this fall. Is supply elastic or inelastic this season? In ten years?

Inelastic this season: growers can only harvest trees already bearing. More elastic in ten years: they can plant more.

3 · Prices. Population growth raises demand by the same amount for beachfront homes and for new cars. In which market does price rise more?

Beachfront homes. Their supply is very inelastic (no new beach), so the demand increase shows up mostly as a higher price. Car makers can build more, so quantity absorbs more of it.
Supply is more elastic with more time, spare capacity, and inputs that are easy to get.
Unit 6 · who really pays a tax

The less elastic side bears more of the tax

A beach town adds a $0.50 tax on every ice-cream cone, collected from the shops. Before the tax a cone sold for $3.00. After the tax, buyers pay $3.30 and shops keep $2.80 per cone.

1. How much of the $0.50 do buyers bear? Sellers?

Buyers pay $0.30 more (60%); sellers keep $0.20 less (40%).

2. Which side of the market is less elastic?

Buyers (demand). The less elastic side bears more of the tax.

3. Would the split change if the town collected the tax from buyers instead of shops?

No. Who writes the check does not decide who bears the tax; the elasticities do.
Unit 6 · who pays a tax · on a graph

Compare each side's price change with the tax

A $2 tax per unit, collected from sellers. Dashed green: supply plus the tax.

1. Read the graph: how much more do buyers pay? How much less do sellers keep?

Buyers: $6.00 − $5.50 = $0.50. Sellers: $5.50 − $4.00 = $1.50. Sellers bear 75% of the $2 tax.

2. Which curve is less elastic at the starting point?

Supply (the steep one): sellers cannot easily cut output, so they absorb most of the tax.

3 · Quick fire. Who bears more? (a) Es = 0.5, Ed = −0.5   (b) demand perfectly inelastic   (c) Es = 0.2, Ed = −2

(a) Split equally: the two sides are equally elastic. (b) Buyers bear all of it. (c) Sellers bear most: supply is far less elastic.

Unit 8a · from production to cost

Smaller MP per worker → higher MC per loaf

A bakery with one oven pays each worker $120 a day.

Worker1234
Extra loaves (MP)40302010
MC per loaf$3$4$6$12

1. Fill in the cost of each extra loaf (MC = wage ÷ MP).

$120 ÷ 40 = $3; ÷ 30 = $4; ÷ 20 = $6; ÷ 10 = $12.

2. Why does MC rise, even though the wage never changes?

The bakery has one oven (a fixed input). Each extra worker adds fewer loaves (diminishing marginal returns), so each extra loaf takes more paid labor.

3. Holiday orders double and the owner adds workers this week. What happens to MC?

It rises: in the short run more output means crowding the same oven.

Unit 8b · the firm in a competitive market

For a price taker, P = MR = its demand

1. Jo sells soybeans at the going price of $11 a bushel. What is her marginal revenue from the 500th bushel?

$11. A price taker sells every bushel at the market price, so MR = P.

2. Jo posts a price of $11.50. How many bushels does she sell?

None. Buyers can get identical soybeans from other farms at $11.

3 · True or false? Market demand for soybeans is inelastic, so each soybean farmer also faces inelastic demand.

False. The market demand curve slopes down, but one farmer's demand is horizontal at the going price: perfectly elastic.

Unit 8b · compare P with ATC, then with AVC

Two thresholds: min ATC and min AVC

A dairy farm in a competitive market: minimum AVC = $14 per hundredweight; minimum ATC = $19. What should it do at each price this month?
P = $22

Produce: economic profit (P > min ATC).

P = $17

Keep producing at a loss: P > min AVC, so sales cover variable cost and part of the fixed cost.

P = $12

Shut down in the short run: P < min AVC. Producing would lose more than the fixed cost.

Then: name the break-even price and the shutdown price.

Break-even = min ATC = $19. Shutdown = min AVC = $14.
Unit 8b · two ways to count cost

Economic profit subtracts implicit costs too

Rosa runs a food cart. Revenue this year: $90,000. Ingredients, permits and cart rental: $55,000. To run the cart she turned down a job paying $42,000.

1. Accounting profit?

TR − explicit = $90,000 − $55,000 = $35,000.

2. Economic profit?

Also subtract the implicit cost: $35,000 − $42,000 = −$7,000. The job would have paid more.

3 · True or false? A firm's economic profit can be larger than its accounting profit.

False. Economic profit = accounting profit − implicit costs, and implicit costs are never negative.

4. What does zero economic profit mean?

A normal profit: the owner earns exactly what the next-best use of her time and money would pay. Accounting profit is still positive.
Unit 8b · entry, exit and the long run

Profit invites entry; losses trigger exit

Microgreens catch on with restaurants. Growers in this competitive market earn positive economic profit. What happens over time?
1New growers enter
2Market supply shifts right
3The price falls
4Profit shrinks until P = min ATC: zero economic profit

Then: A county rule caps the number of growers' licenses. Can economic profit last in the long run now? Why?

Yes, it can. The cap is a barrier to entry: without free entry nothing pushes the price down to min ATC.

And: Suppose instead growers were losing money. What happens in the long run?

Some growers exit; supply shifts left; the price rises until the remaining growers earn zero economic profit.
