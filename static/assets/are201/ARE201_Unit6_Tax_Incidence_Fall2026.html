LO 6.7 + LO 6.9 · a tax you already pay

Every gallon of gas carries a tax. Who actually pays it?

How the law is written

Federal and North Carolina motor fuels taxes are collected from the supplier, not from you at the pump. No line on your receipt says “tax.”

Vote before we draw anything. Of each dollar of gasoline tax, who ends up worse off by more?

Commit to an answer.

Remember the elasticity lecture: the price elasticity of gasoline demand over a month came out at 0.28.

LO 6.7 · two pictures of the same tax

You can draw a tax two ways. They give identical answers.

Way 1 · shift the supply curve

A $1 tax is a $1 addition to the seller's cost of every unit, so supply shifts up by exactly $1.

Way 2 · drive a wedge

Leave both curves alone. Find the quantity where the vertical gap between them is exactly $1.

Same quantity traded, same price to buyers, same price to sellers. We will use Way 2 for the rest of today—it makes the split visible, which is the whole point.

Your textbook draws it the same way—the supply shift is left out there too, for the same reason.

LO 6.7 · what Rounds 2 and 3 showed

Who writes the check does not determine who bears the burden

Tax the seller $1.00

Supply shifts up by $1.00. The market price becomes $3.80.

  • Buyers pay $3.80
  • Sellers collect $3.80, remit $1.00, keep $2.80
  • Quantity: 75 million

Tax the buyer $1.00

Demand shifts down by $1.00. The market price becomes $2.80.

  • Buyers pay $2.80 plus $1.00 tax = $3.80
  • Sellers keep $2.80
  • Quantity: 75 million
Identical outcome. The law decides who remits the money—that is statutory incidence. The market decides who is actually made worse off—that is economic incidence. Only the second one matters for welfare.

So “we will tax the oil companies, not drivers” is a claim about the wrong kind of incidence.

LO 6.7 · the rule behind Rounds 4 and 5

The more inelastic side of the market bears more of the tax

If demand is more inelastic than supply, buyers bear most of the burden.
If supply is more inelastic than demand, sellers bear most of the burden.

Gasoline, one month

Buyers cannot change car, commute, or home by Friday. Sellers can redirect fuel to other markets. Buyers are the trapped side.

The same tax over ten years

Buyers get a more efficient car, move closer to work, change jobs. The trapped side becomes less trapped, and the buyers' share falls.

Round 4

Most buyers valued the good far above 60, so few were close to walking away. Many sellers had costs just under 60 and quit as soon as their price slipped. Demand was much less elastic than supply, so the price buyers paid had to rise.

Round 5

The mirror image: buyers crowded near the margin, sellers did not. Supply was much less elastic than demand, so the price sellers kept had to fall.

Many traders near the margin = many escape routes = the more elastic side. What decides the split is the comparison between the two sides, not either side alone.
LO 6.7 · predict before revealing

Two excise taxes. Predict who bears each one.

A · Cigarettes

A new tax on cigarette manufacturers. Adult smokers' demand elasticity is about 0.3. Producers can adjust output freely.

Buyers bear most. Demand is far more inelastic than supply, so the tax mostly shows up as a higher shelf price with little drop in quantity. Good for revenue; weak as a way to reduce smoking.

B · Restaurant meals

A new tax on restaurants. Demand elasticity is about 2.27—diners can cook at home. Restaurants have limited ability to exit quickly.

Sellers bear most. Demand is highly elastic, so raising menu prices drives customers away. Much of the tax comes out of the restaurant's margin—and quantity falls a lot.
Commit before revealing.
LO 6.7 · the trades that disappeared · back to Unit 5

Revenue collected, and trades destroyed

Revenue = tax × Qtraded

$1.00 × 75 million = $75 million. A transfer—it moves from buyers and sellers to the government, and nothing is destroyed.

Deadweight loss

The 15 million gallons between 75 and 90 were worth more to buyers than they cost sellers. Those trades no longer happen, and nobody gets that surplus—not even the government.

Exactly the Unit 5 distinction: transfers move surplus; lost trades destroy it.

Corollary: the more inelastic both sides are, the smaller the deadweight loss and the larger the revenue. That is the efficiency case for taxing inelastic goods—and it collides with the fairness case, since inelastic goods are often necessities.

LO 6.7 · what to take away

Three lessons from our market

1

Who writes the check does not decide who bears the tax

Round 2 vs Round 3

Statutory incidence is set by law. Economic incidence is set by the market.

2

The more inelastic side bears more

Round 4 vs Round 5

Find the trapped side: the one with fewer escape routes absorbs more of the tax.

3

A tax destroys trades, not just money

Round 1 vs every taxed round

Revenue is a transfer. The trades that no longer happen are deadweight loss.

The one-line version: find the trapped side. It bears most of the tax, no matter who writes the check.

Practice 1 · Who bears the burden?

Apply the incidence rule

Alcohol

Demand for alcohol is highly inelastic. The burden of a higher alcohol tax falls mainly on…

Buyers have the fewest escape routes, so they absorb most of the tax.

Fuel, at the extremes

A fuel tax is imposed where the price elasticity of supply is 0 and demand is perfectly elastic. Who pays the tax?

Buyers leave at any price above the market price, and sellers cannot change quantity at all—so sellers bear the entire tax.

The law decides who writes the check. Elasticity decides who bears the burden.

Practice 3 · Revenue or behavior?

Same tax, two different goals

Goal: raise revenue

A city wants the most revenue from one tax, and a tax works like a price increase. Using these published |Ed| values, which good should it tax?

The most inelastic good: quantity barely falls, so the tax keeps its base (holding market size equal).

Goal: change behavior

A state taxes junk food so that people eat less of it. For the tax to actually deter consumption, demand for junk food should be…

Only elastic demand makes quantity fall a lot. The catch: that same response shrinks the revenue the tax collects.

Raise revenue → tax inelastic goods. Change behavior → the tax works only if demand is elastic.
Exit ticket · LO 6.7

One tax, three questions

A $0.40 per-gallon tax, where demand is inelastic and supply is elastic.

(a) Who bears more, and why?

(b) Much or little revenue?

(c) After ten years, is the buyers' share bigger or smaller?

(a) Buyers—they are the more inelastic side, so they have the fewest ways to escape the tax. (b) A lot of revenue, because inelastic demand means quantity barely falls, and revenue is tax × quantity traded. (c) Smaller. Over ten years buyers find substitutes—different vehicles, homes, commutes—so demand becomes more elastic and the burden shifts toward sellers.
