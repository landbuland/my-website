International Trade & Tariffs: Review

From the world price to who really pays a tariff

Part A · Free trade · recap

Start every trade graph the same way

Compare the world price (PW) with the no-trade (autarky) price, where domestic supply meets domestic demand.

PW below the no-trade price

The country imports: imports = Qd − Qs.

Buyers gain (pay less, buy more). Domestic sellers lose.

PW above the no-trade price

The country exports: exports = Qs − Qd.

Sellers gain (sell more at a higher price). Domestic buyers lose.

Gains from trade

The triangle between PW and the no-trade point.

Winners gain more than losers lose, so total surplus rises. That does not mean everyone is better off.

Why trade at all? Each country produces where its opportunity cost is lowest (comparative advantage), even if one country is better at everything.
Part A · Comparative advantage · Q1

Better at both goods: still trade?

In a given period, Argentina can produce 1,000 barrels of oil or 800 pounds of beef. Japan can produce 200 barrels of oil or 100 pounds of beef. A policymaker in Argentina argues that, since Argentina is better at producing both goods, it has no incentive to trade with Japan. Is the policymaker correct?

Answer: A · no.

One barrel of oil costs Argentina 0.8 lb of beef, but costs Japan only 0.5 lb, so Japan has the comparative advantage in oil. Argentina gives up 1.25 barrels per pound of beef, Japan 2, so Argentina has it in beef. Absolute advantage does not decide who should make what.

Part A · Imports · Q2

What do imports do to an economy?

Based on the concepts in this course, which is the most accurate statement about the effects of imports, from an economist’s perspective?

Answer: B.

Imports lower the domestic price to PW: consumer surplus rises by more than producer surplus falls. Domestic producers do lose, so imports create winners and losers, but the country as a whole gains.

Part A · World price vs. no-trade price · Q3

Four quick calls

Decide each one before revealing it.

(a) At the world price, domestic demand is greater than domestic supply. Does the country import or export?

Import. Imports fill the gap: imports = Qd − Qs.

(b) A country opens to trade and PW is above the no-trade price. Exports or imports?

Exports. At the higher price, sellers make more than buyers here want: exports = Qs − Qd.

(c) In that same case (PW above the no-trade price), does consumer surplus increase or decrease?

Decrease. Domestic buyers now pay the higher world price and buy less. Producer surplus rises by more.

(d) PW is below the no-trade price. At PW, what develops in the domestic market?A. a surplus · B. nothing · C. a shortage

C. A shortage: at the lower price Qd > Qs. Imports cover exactly that shortage.

Part A · Winners and losers · Q4

Which statements about trade are true?

Select all of the following statements that are true.

Answer: A, B and C.

Gains from trade mean total surplus rises: the winners gain more than the losers lose. Some groups (sellers who now face imports, their workers) are still worse off.

Part A · Gains from trade · Q5

Which area is the gain from trade?

Before trade the market is at point A. The country then imports at the world price PW. True or false: area Z (the purple triangle) represents the gains from trade.

Answer: True.

Before trade: CS = W, PS = X + Y. After trade: CS = W + X + Z, PS = Y. Area X only moves from sellers to buyers; Z is new surplus, the gain from trade.

Part A · Exports, by the numbers · Q6

Baskets: open to trade

Domestic supply and demand for baskets. The world price is $10.

(a) Will this country import or export baskets?

Export. $10 is above the no-trade price of $7.

(b) How many baskets are exported?

Qs − Qd = 105 − 40 = 65.

(c) Calculate the gains from trade.

½ × ($10 − $7) × 65 = $97.50.

(d) Calculate producer surplus with trade.

½ × 105 × ($10 − $1) = $472.50.

(e) Who loses, and how much?

Domestic buyers: CS falls from $245 to $80 (−$165). PS rises by $262.50, so the country gains $97.50.

Part A · Imports, by the numbers · Q7

Imports at a world price of $6

Domestic supply and demand. The world price is $6.

(a) Is the country importing or exporting?

Importing: $6 is below the no-trade price of $9.

(b) How much does it import?

Qd − Qs = 8 − 2 = 6.

(c) How large is consumer surplus at $6?

½ × 8 × ($14 − $6) = $32.

(d) How large is producer surplus at $6?

½ × 2 × ($6 − $4) = $2.

(e) How large are the gains from trade?

½ × ($9 − $6) × 6 = $9. Check: total surplus $25 → $34.

Part B · Tariff effects · recap

One tariff, four areas

PT = PW + tariff  ·  tariff = PT − PW

Quantities: Qs rises, Qd falls, imports shrink from both ends.

Consumers: lose A + B + C + D

Producers: gain A

Government revenue: C = tariff × imports after the tariff

DWL = B + D

B = production inefficiency (units made here that cost more than PW). D = consumption inefficiency (purchases worth more than PW that no longer happen).
Part B · Tariff effects · Q8

True or false: four quick statements

Decide each one before revealing it.

(a) A tariff is meant to decrease imports.

True. The higher domestic price raises Qs and lowers Qd, so imports fall from both ends.

(b) A tariff is meant to help producers.

True. Domestic producers gain area A. Consumers lose more than that.

(c) In a tariff graph, the amount of the tariff is PT − PW.

True. The tariff is the gap between the two price lines.

(d) Domestic producers do not pay the tariff, so they keep selling at PW.

False. The domestic price rises to PT for every unit sold here, imported or not, so domestic producers also sell at PT.

Part B · Tariff effects · Q9

Consumer surplus before the tariff

Before the tariff, at the world price (PW), domestic consumers had a consumer surplus equal to:

Answer: B.

At PW consumers buy QD, so their surplus runs all the way down to PW. The tariff takes away A + B + C + D and leaves only the blue triangle.

Part B · Tariff welfare, by the numbers · Q10

Same market, now a tariff: price rises to $8

World price $6, tariff price $8.

(a) How large is the tariff?

$8 − $6 = $2.

(b) Producer surplus after the tariff?

½ × 4 × ($8 − $4) = $8 (was $2).

(c) Consumer surplus after the tariff?

½ × 6 × ($14 − $8) = $18 (was $32).

(d) Production inefficiency (B)?

½ × (4 − 2) × $2 = $2.

(e) Deadweight loss?

B + D = $2 + ½ × (8 − 6) × $2 = $4.

(f) Government revenue?

$2 × (6 − 4) = $4. Check: CS falls $14 = A $6 + B $2 + C $4 + D $2.

Part B · Tariff welfare, by the numbers · Q11

A $1 tariff

The no-trade price is $4. The world price is $2, and the market currently trades at $2. Producers lobby for a $1 tariff.

(a) Consumer surplus at the world price?

½ × 10 × ($12 − $2) = $50.

(b) Producer surplus at the world price?

½ × 4 × $2 = $4.

(c) Producer surplus with the tariff?

½ × 6 × $3 = $9.

(d) Consumer surplus with the tariff?

½ × 9 × ($12 − $3) = $40.50.

(e) Deadweight loss with the tariff?

B = ½ × (6 − 4) × $1 = $1; D = ½ × (10 − 9) × $1 = $0.50. DWL = $1.50.

Part B · Tariff welfare, by the numbers · Q12

World price $20, tariff $10

The graph shows domestic supply (SD) and demand. The world price is $20 per unit. A tariff of $10 per unit is introduced.

(a) How much will be imported?

At $30: Qd − Qs = 40 − 30 = 10.

(b) Government (tariff) revenue?

$10 × 10 = $100.

(c) Deadweight loss?

B = ½ × (30 − 20) × $10 = $50; D = ½ × (60 − 40) × $10 = $100. DWL = $150.

(d) Consumer surplus with the tariff?

½ × 40 × ($50 − $30) = $400.

Part B · Tariff welfare, by the numbers · Q13

Import price plus tariff: $35

The world price is $20. With the tariff, the import price is $35 (PT on the graph).

(a) At $20: exports? imports?

Exports 0; imports 850 − 100 = 750.

(b) How large is the tariff?

$35 − $20 = $15.

(c) Government revenue after the tariff?

$15 × (650 − 300) = $5,250.

(d) Deadweight loss?

½ × 200 × $15 + ½ × 200 × $15 = $3,000.

(e) Consumption inefficiency part of the DWL?

D = ½ × (850 − 650) × $15 = $1,500.

(f) Increase in producer surplus?

A = ½ × (100 + 300) × $15 = $3,000.

Part B · Tariff welfare, by the numbers · Q14

From no trade to free trade to a tariff

Quantities are in thousands, so every area is in thousands of dollars.

(a) CS with no trade (autarky)?

½ × 25 × ($460 − $310) = $1,875 thousand.

(b) CS with free trade at $250?

½ × 35 × ($460 − $250) = $3,675 thousand.

(c) Volume of imports at $250?

35 − 15 = 20 thousand.

(d) Imports now cost $280. Size of the tariff?

$280 − $250 = $30.

(e) Consumer surplus with the tariff?

½ × 30 × ($460 − $280) = $2,700 thousand.

(f) Deadweight loss with the tariff?

½ × 5 × $30 + ½ × 5 × $30 = $150 thousand.

Part B · Removing a tariff · Q15

What if the tariff is removed?

A government ends its tariffs on another country’s goods. What is the expected effect in the domestic market?

(a) Consumer surplus would ____.

Increase by A + B + C + D: the price falls back to PW.

(b) Producer surplus would ____.

Decrease by A: domestic sellers face cheaper imports again.

(c) Government revenue would ____.

Decrease to zero: area C was the tariff revenue.

(d) Deadweight loss would ____.

Decrease: B + D disappear, so total surplus rises.

Part B · Long-run effects · Q16

High tariffs, year after year

What is likely to be a long-term effect of a country consistently maintaining high tariffs on most imports? Select all that apply.

Answer: B and C.

Protected from cheaper imports, domestic firms face less pressure to cut costs (area B is that inefficiency, every year). Buyers keep paying PT and have fewer imported products to choose from.

Part C · Tariffs in practice · recap

Why tariffs, and who really pays?

Why use them?

Protect an industry and its jobs; answer dumping; producer pressure.

The gain is concentrated on a few sellers; the loss is spread over many buyers.

Who writes the check?

The domestic importer pays the tariff to its own customs office.

Paying the check is not the same as bearing the cost (Unit 6).

Who bears it?

The side that cannot get away.

If the world price stays put, domestic buyers bear all of it. Foreign sellers bear part only if they cut their price.

Studies of the 2018 US tariffs: prices of the taxed imports rose by about the full tariff. In 2025–26, Canadian lumber mills, with nowhere else to sell, absorbed much of the US duties.
Part C · Why tariffs · Q17

Why protect an industry?

Why would a government most likely implement protectionist policies to protect its domestic industries from imports?

Answer: C.

Protection shields domestic sellers (area A) and their workers. It does the opposite of A: imported goods cost more.

Part C · Who pays · Q18

A 50% tariff on Brazilian coffee

Suppose the US puts a 50% tariff on coffee imported from Brazil, one of its largest suppliers. Coffee from other countries is not taxed.

(a) The most likely short-run effect in the US?A. lower retail prices · B. higher prices, and buyers switch to coffee from other countries · C. a guaranteed increase in supply

B. Brazilian coffee now costs importers more, so US prices rise and roasters buy more from untaxed origins.

(b) Who are the most likely short-run losers?A. Brazilian exporters only · B. US consumers and the Brazilian exporters hit by the tariff · C. coffee growers in other countries · D. China

B. US buyers pay more. Brazilian exporters lose US sales or must cut their price to sell; how the cost splits depends on which side can get away. Growers elsewhere gain sales.

Part C · Who pays · Q19

“China pays the tariffs, not the United States.”

Using the tools from this unit, how would you respond? Write three or four sentences before revealing the model answer.

(a) Who writes the check, and who bears the cost?

The US importer pays the tariff to US Customs. If Chinese sellers keep their price (the world price does not change), the US price rises by the full tariff, so US buyers bear it: consumer surplus falls by A + B + C + D. Part of that goes to US producers (A) and the US government (C); B + D is lost. Chinese sellers bear part only if they cut their prices. Studies of the 2018 tariffs found US import prices rose by about the full tariff.

Wrap-up · before Exam 3

Five checks for every trade question

1 · Which case?

PW below the no-trade price: imports = Qd − Qs. Above it: exports = Qs − Qd.

2 · Gains from trade

The triangle between PW and the no-trade point. Total surplus rises; not everyone wins.

3 · Read the new price

PT = PW + tariff. Read the new Qs and Qd at PT.

4 · Areas

Revenue = tariff × imports after the tariff. DWL = B + D: two triangles, one on each side.

5 · Who pays? The importer writes the check; buyers bear the cost when the world price does not fall.
