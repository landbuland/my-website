Q1 · Measure it · Warm-up

Price falls 4%. Quantity demanded rises 8%.

What is the price elasticity of demand?

Answer: A. −2, which we report as |Ed| = 2 → elastic.

Why: 8% ÷ 4% = 2. The raw ratio is negative because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions. “2%” is wrong: an elasticity is a ratio of two percentages, so it has no units. 0.5 divides the wrong way.

Q2 · Measure it · From words

Wheat: the price rises from $10 to $12

The market

The price of a bushel of wheat rises from $10 to $12. Quantity demanded falls from 1,000 to 800 bushels.

Use the midpoint method to find |Ed|. Is demand elastic or inelastic?
%ΔQ = (Q2 − Q1) ÷ [(Q1 + Q2) ÷ 2]
%ΔP = (P2 − P1) ÷ [(P1 + P2) ÷ 2]
1
Averages: Q = (1,000 + 800) ÷ 2 = 900 · P = ($10 + $12) ÷ 2 = $11Use the same two bases whichever way the price moves.
2
%ΔQ = 200 ÷ 900 = 22.2% · %ΔP = $2 ÷ $11 = 18.2%Drop the signs: we report the absolute value.
3
|Ed| = 22.2% ÷ 18.2% = 1.22 → elasticQuantity responds more than price in percentage terms.

Q7 · Explain it · Determinants

Locally grown organic vegetables

Which change would make the demand for locally grown organic vegetables more elastic?

Answer: A. More close substitutes → more elastic demand.

Why: cheaper imported organics give buyers somewhere to go when local prices rise. B is a cost change on the supply side. C makes the good feel like a necessity, which makes demand less elastic. D makes the closest alternative more expensive, leaving fewer good substitutes.

Q8 · Explain it · Rapid round

Elastic or inelastic? Four quick calls

Substitutes

A good with many close substitutes

Elastic. When its price rises, buyers can switch easily.
Necessity or luxury

A luxury good

Elastic. A luxury is easy to skip or postpone.
Share of income

An item that takes 67% of your income

Elastic. A purchase that big is worth shopping around over.
Time horizon

Two estimates for gasoline, 0.4 and 1.8. Which is the short-run one?

0.4. In the short run, drivers cannot change cars, commutes, or homes.
When the determinants disagree—housing is a large budget share and a necessity—the measured number settles it.
Q9 · Revenue · Read the revenue signal

Corn: price and revenue in two different years

Year A

The price of corn rises. Farm revenue rises. Consumer demand for corn must be…

Price and revenue moved together, so the price effect won: demand is inelastic.

Year B

The price of corn falls. Farm revenue rises. Consumer demand for corn must be…

Price and revenue moved in opposite directions, so the quantity effect won: demand is elastic.

Shortcut: P and TR move together → inelastic · opposite directions → elastic · TR unchanged → unit elastic.
Q10 · Revenue · Predict, then check

A farm stand raises its apple price 10%

The manager estimates |Ed| = 1.5 for organic apples. After a 10% price increase, quantity sold falls by ____ and total revenue ____.

Answer: C. Quantity falls 15%, and total revenue falls.

Why: 1.5 × 10% = 15%. Demand is elastic, so the lost sales outweigh the higher price. Check with illustrative numbers: $4.00 × 100 lb = $400 before, $4.40 × 85 lb = $374 after.

Q11 · Revenue · True or false?

Four statements that trip people up

aAlong a straight-line demand curve, the price elasticity of demand is the same at every point.

False. The slope is constant; elasticity is not. Elastic on the upper half, unit elastic at the midpoint, inelastic below.

bTotal revenue is highest where |Ed| = 1.

True. Above that point a price cut raises revenue; below it a price rise does. Revenue peaks where demand is unit elastic.

cRaising the price always increases total revenue.

False. Only when demand is inelastic. With elastic demand, revenue falls.

d“I would pay any amount for my morning coffee.” For this buyer, |Ed| = 0.

True. Quantity does not respond to price at all: perfectly inelastic, a vertical demand curve.
Q12 · Sellers · Supply elasticity

Milk: price and quantity supplied both rise

The market

The price of milk rises from $3.90 to $4.10 per gallon. Quantity supplied rises from 10,000 to 14,000 gallons.

Use the midpoint method to find Es. Is supply elastic or inelastic?
Es = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP
same midpoint bases as on the demand side
1
Averages: Q = 12,000 gallons · P = $4.00
2
%ΔQs = 4,000 ÷ 12,000 = 33.3% · %ΔP = $0.20 ÷ $4.00 = 5.0%
3
Es = 33.3% ÷ 5.0% = 6.67 → elasticPositive, because price and quantity supplied move together—no absolute value needed.
Q13 · Sellers · Time and supply

Christmas trees: why supply can’t keep up

The market

Real Christmas tree prices have risen because of supply shortages.

(a) Which is the most likely short-run price elasticity of supply for real Christmas trees?

0.1: very inelastic supply. This season’s trees were planted years ago, so quantity supplied can barely respond to the higher price.

(b) Why is short-run supply so inelastic?

A tree takes 8–12 years to grow to market size. Shipping costs or shoppers switching to artificial trees do not explain the sellers’ side.

(c) If high prices last for years, what happens to Es?

It rises. Growers plant more, and as those trees mature, quantity supplied can respond: long-run supply is more elastic.
Q14 · Related goods · Read the headline

Two headlines, two signs

“In Florida, rising menu prices for UberEats and GrubHub have pumped up demand for DoorDash.”

Which elasticity concept is most relevant?

One good’s price changed the quantity demanded of another, in the same direction: Ecross > 0, so UberEats/GrubHub and DoorDash are substitutes.

“In the Midwest, rising gas prices dent demand for tractors.”

The cross-price elasticity here is negative because…

Gas up → tractor demand down: opposite directions, Ecross < 0. Goods used together are complements.

Q15 · Taxes · Who bears the burden?

Apply the incidence rule

Alcohol

Demand for alcohol is highly inelastic. The burden of a higher alcohol tax falls mainly on…

Buyers have the fewest escape routes, so they absorb most of the tax.

Fuel, at the extremes

A fuel tax is imposed where the price elasticity of supply is 0 and demand is perfectly elastic. Who pays the tax?

Buyers leave at any price above the market price, and sellers cannot change quantity at all—so sellers bear the entire tax.

The law decides who writes the check. Elasticity decides who bears the burden.

Q17 · Taxes · Revenue or behavior?

Same tax, two different goals

Goal: raise revenue

A city wants the most revenue from one tax, and a tax works like a price increase. Using the published |Ed| values, which good should it tax?

The most inelastic good: quantity barely falls, so the tax keeps its base (holding market size equal).

Goal: change behavior

A state taxes junk food so that people eat less of it. For the tax to actually deter consumption, demand for junk food should be…

Only elastic demand makes quantity fall a lot. The catch: that same response shrinks the revenue the tax collects.

Raise revenue → tax inelastic goods. Change behavior → the tax works only if demand is elastic.

Q19 · Farm income · Select all that apply

Fertilizer gets cheaper and corn revenue rises

Fertilizer costs fall significantly, and corn farmers’ total revenue rises. What must have happened?

ASupply shifted to an inelastic part of the demand curve.

False. On an inelastic part, a lower price would have cut revenue.

BSupply shifted to an elastic part of the demand curve.

True. Revenue rose even though price fell, so the quantity effect won.

CIn percentage terms, the change in price exceeded the change in quantity demanded.

False. That is what inelastic demand looks like.

DIn percentage terms, the change in price was less than the change in quantity demanded.

True. |%ΔQ| > |%ΔP| is the definition of elastic.
Cheaper fertilizer shifts supply right: price falls, quantity rises. Compare Q18: there demand was inelastic, so revenue moved opposite to the harvest. Here it is elastic, so revenue moves with it.
Q20 · Put it all together · Select all that apply

One good, four elasticities

Good X: |Ed| = 2 · Ecross (X with Y) = −3 · Es = 1 · Eincome = 4

AGood X is a normal good.

True. Eincome = 4 > 0 (above 1, a luxury).

BGood X is an inferior good.

False. An inferior good has Eincome < 0.

CIf the price of good Y rises 5%, the quantity demanded of X falls 15%.

True. −3 × 5% = −15%: X and Y are complements.

DIf the price of X rises 5%, the quantity supplied of X rises 5%.

True. Es = 1: quantity supplied moves in proportion.

EIf the price of X rises 5%, total revenue from X rises.

False. |Ed| = 2 is elastic: quantity falls 10%, so revenue falls.

FThis market sits at the midpoint of a straight-line demand curve.

False. The midpoint is unit elastic; |Ed| = 2 is on the upper, elastic part.
Before you leave

Six habits prevent most elasticity mistakes

Use the midpoint, every time

Average P and average Q are the bases, so the answer is the same in both directions.

MEASURE

Compare |E| to 1

That one comparison gives both the label and the total-revenue rule.

CLASSIFY

Say the number as a sentence

|Ed| = 0.7: a 10% price rise cuts quantity demanded 7%. Inelastic is not zero.

INTERPRET

Read the sign

Income: + normal, − inferior. Cross: + substitutes, − complements. Supply: always +.

SIGNS

Find the trapped side

The more inelastic side bears more of a tax, no matter who writes the check.

TAXES

Supply shocks meet inelastic food demand

Price moves more than quantity, so farm revenue moves against the size of the harvest.
