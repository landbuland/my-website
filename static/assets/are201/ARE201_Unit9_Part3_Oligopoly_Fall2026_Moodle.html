Meatpacking: four companies, most of the beef

Oligopoly: a few firms sell most of a market’s output, so each must think about how its rivals will react.
The four largest U.S. beef packers (real data)

1 · A few big firms

Four companies slaughter about 85% of the cattle fed for beef.

2 · High barriers

A big plant costs a fortune, and economies of scale favor the biggest (Monday’s reason 3).

3 · Strategic interdependence

What one packer earns depends on what the others do. A sardine boat or the only vet never has to ask, “What will my rival do?”

The share is of steer and heifer slaughter, not of all beef sold: how you define the market matters. The same packers are also the few buyers of cattle (8b’s cannery): an oligopsony.

Data: USDA GIPSA, as reported by USDA ERS (incl. AER-785, table 3) and, for 2015, Western Livestock Journal (2021); rounded.

Closer to perfect competition
A cartel is a group of firms that agree to sell the monopoly quantity at the monopoly price. Overt collusion: they agree openly. Tacit collusion: they never meet, but each holds back, expecting the others to do the same.
In the U.S., agreeing with rivals on price or output is illegal (Sherman Act, 1890).

Two packers sell beef in one region. Each decides on its own: hold output at 30 or expand to 40. What each earns depends on what both do.

Nash equilibrium
Why collude? Each packer’s own best choice leaves both worse off: a prisoner’s dilemma. An agreement to hold is worth $200k a week to each. The catch: each still earns more by breaking it ($2,000k), so cartels are hard to keep.
1
If B holds 30A earns $1,800k by holding, $2,000k by expanding → expand.
2
If B expands to 40A earns $1,500k by holding, $1,600k by expanding → expand. Either way: expanding is A’s dominant strategy.
3
B is in the same spot: both expandNash equilibrium: $1,600k each. Neither can do better by changing only its own choice.
4
If both held 30: $1,800k each$200k more for each. Together they sell 60, the monopoly amount, and earn $3,600k, the most this market can earn.
Made-up numbers: market demand P = $140 − Q, cost $20 a unit. A classroom example: in a real market, agreeing with a rival on output is against the law.

Colluding: two packers, one monopoly

Beef market, both packers (made-up numbers)
1
Sell what a monopoly wouldTogether they pick the total where MR = MC: 60 units, 30 each. Competing, they sold 80.
2
The price risesFewer units for sale move the price up the demand curve: $60 → $80.
3
Split the profit($80 − $20) × 60 = $3,600k, the monopoly profit. Half each: $1,800k, up from $1,600k.
Collusion = rivals acting as one monopoly: less output, a higher price, and the monopoly profit shared. Buyers pay $20 more a unit and get 20 fewer units.
Made-up numbers, as on the previous page: market demand P = $140 − Q, cost $20 a unit. “Compete” = both expand to 40, the previous page’s Nash equilibrium.

Which grocery markets are oligopolies?

Vote first

Which of these U.S. markets are oligopolies? Select all that apply.

AFresh carrots✓ Answer Two companies (Grimmway and Bolthouse) grow at least 60% of U.S. carrots.
BPrepared soup✓ Answer The top four sell about 70%; Campbell’s alone, about 39%.
CRefrigerated almond milk✓ Answer The top four sell about 81%; Almond Breeze and Silk alone, about 73%.
DDip for chips✓ Answer PepsiCo (Tostitos, Lay’s, Fritos) alone sells about 88%: close to a monopoly.
EFresh tomatoesNo Many farms grow tomatoes, and no small group of companies sells most of them.
The test: do a few firms sell most of the market? Add up the top four firms’ shares (the four-firm concentration ratio, CR4); above about 60% is a tight oligopoly. Even a farm crop like carrots can be one.

Shipping less, charging the same

Vote first

The two companies that sell most of the fertilizer to North Carolina farms agree to ship less and to charge the same price. This is most likely:

AProduct differentiationNo That is making your product a little different from your rivals’. These two are making a deal, not standing apart.
BPrice discriminationNo That is one seller charging different buyers different prices for the same product. Here every farm pays one price.
CCollusion in an oligopoly✓ Answer A few sellers agree to sell less so the price rises: a cartel, acting like one monopoly (page 6).
DNon-price competition in monopolistic competitionNo That is many sellers competing with ads, service, or style. These two are not competing at all.
Two signs of collusion: a few sellers, and an agreement to sell less or charge more. In the U.S., that agreement is illegal (Sherman Act).

Which one is overt collusion?

Vote first

In which situation does overt collusion take place?

ASmaller firms never talk to the largest firm, but always charge whatever it charges.No That is tacit collusion: no agreement, just following the leader.
BFirms openly agree on price and output, and act together to earn a monopoly’s profit.✓ Answer An open agreement: a cartel.
CMany small firms compete, and their prices are similar but slightly different.No That is monopolistic competition (Wednesday): no agreement at all.
DMany small firms compete, and the market price stays stable.No That is perfect competition (8b): price takers, no agreement.
Overt collusion: they agree openly. Tacit collusion: they never agree, but each holds back, expecting the others to do the same (page 3). Either way, the price ends up higher than under competition.

What counts as breaking the deal?

Vote first

Packers A and B agreed to hold output so beef sells at $80 (page 6). Which moves by Packer A break the deal? Select all that apply.

AQuietly sells to a big grocery chain at $75✓ Answer A secret price cut: it takes sales from B.
BLaunches a new line of pre-seasoned steaks✓ Answer A new product pulls B’s customers: non-price competition.
CRuns a big TV ad campaign✓ Answer Ads pull B’s customers too: non-price competition.
DSwitches to new accounting softwareNo An inside change: it takes no customers from B.
EGives its own workers a raiseNo An inside cost: it takes no customers from B.
Cheating is any move that wins customers from the partner: a lower price, or non-price competition (ads, new products, better service). So a cartel has to watch more than the price.

Suppose the few companies that make most of the breakfast cereal sold in the U.S. secretly agreed on prices. What would most likely happen?

AMore kinds of cereal on the shelvesNo Nothing in the deal adds products, and with less competition there is less push to try new ones.
BLower cereal pricesNo The whole point of the deal is a higher price.
CTougher competition among the cereal makersNo Agreeing on prices is the opposite of competing.
DHigher prices and fewer boxes sold, hurting consumers✓ Answer Like the two packers on page 6: less output, a higher price, and the monopoly profit shared.
