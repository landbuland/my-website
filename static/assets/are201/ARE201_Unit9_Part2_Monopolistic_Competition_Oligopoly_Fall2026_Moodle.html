definitions

What is monopolistic competition?

Monopolistic competition: many firms, each selling a product a little different from the others’, with free entry and exit.

1 · Many sellers

No single brewery is big enough to set the market’s price.

2 · Different products

Each has a little market power: raise the price, and some fans stay.

3 · Easy entry

Profit attracts new rivals: a new taproom can open next door.

North Carolina has more than 400 craft breweries. Each is a small monopoly over its own beer, with hundreds of close substitutes. And since 2024, more NC breweries have closed than opened.

The name says both halves: a little monopoly (your own product), lots of competition (everyone else’s).

Sources: Brewers Association and N.C. Craft Brewers Guild, as reported by Axios Charlotte (2026). Definition: OpenStax, Principles of Economics 2e, Sec. 10.1.

Three firms, three demand curves

the long run · entry and exit

$450 a night attracts new taprooms

Hoppy Owl’s demand (made-up numbers)
Profit: P $8.25 > ATC $6.45. Other brewers see $450 a night, and nothing stops them from opening.
Entry: Night Barn opens next door. Some of Hoppy Owl’s drinkers go there, so Hoppy Owl’s demand and MR shift left. Its profit shrinks.
Long run: taprooms keep opening until Hoppy Owl’s demand just touches ATC: 200 pints at $7.00 = ATC. Zero economic profit, so entry stops.
The mirror: with too many taprooms, P < ATC: a loss. Some close; their drinkers go to the others, so each survivor’s demand shifts right until P = ATC again.
Profit → entry → each firm’s D shifts left → P = ATC
Loss → exit → each firm’s D shifts right → P = ATC
Trap: entry shifts each brewery’s demand, not the market demand for beer.

the long run

Zero profit, but not perfect competition

Hoppy Owl in the long run (made-up numbers)
1
P = ATC = $7.00 → zero economic profitLike 8b: entry competes the profit away. The owners still earn their opportunity cost (normal profit).
2
P $7.00 > MC $4.00 → a little market powerHoppy Owl still picks Q where MR = MC. The last pint is worth $7 to a drinker but costs $4 to pour.
3
200 pints, not 400 → excess capacityPouring 400 a night would bring ATC down to $6.00. Hoppy Owl stops at 200: a half-empty taproom on a Tuesday.
8b, perfect competition, long run: P = MR = MC = minimum ATC. Monopolistic competition: MR = MC and P = ATC, but P > MC, and output is below the lowest-cost level.

Zero economic profit does not mean P = MC or minimum ATC.

is that bad? · hands up

The price of variety

The cost

  • P > MC: some pints worth more than they cost never get poured.
  • Excess capacity: each taproom is too small to reach its lowest ATC.
  • A perfectly competitive “beer coast” would sell one beer, at $6.00 a pint (the lowest ATC).

The benefit

  • Hazy IPA, stout, sour, pilsner, amber: a beer for every taste.
  • Drinkers pay a little more to get the one they like.
Hands up

Would you trade 400 breweries for one beer at $6.00 a pint?

Economists cannot say there is “too much” variety. Buyers clearly pay for some of it.
Advertising tries to shift your own demand right and make it less elastic (fewer drinkers see a close substitute). Some ads inform: a new taproom, prices, a new beer. Some only persuade.
Follows OpenStax 2e, Sec. 10.1 (Monopolistic Competition and Efficiency; The Benefits of Variety and Product Differentiation).

Why can only the monopoly keep its profit?

Vote first

This year, the only vet in a small town (Monday) and Hoppy Owl both earn an economic profit. Five years from now, only the vet still does. Why?

AA second clinic could not cover its costs in a small town, but a new taproom can open next door.✓ Answer A barrier to entry protects the vet’s profit. Free entry competes Hoppy Owl’s away.
BThe vet’s demand slopes down; Hoppy Owl’s demand is flat.No Both slope down. Only a price taker’s demand is flat (8b).
CThe vet sets MR = MC; Hoppy Owl sets P = MC.No Both pick Q where MR = MC, then read P from the demand curve.
DHoppy Owl sells the same beer as its rivals.No Reversed: its beer is a little different. That is why its demand slopes down.
Monopoly and monopolistic competition share the rule (MR = MC), a downward-sloping demand, and P > MC. The difference is entry: blocked for a monopoly, free for monopolistic competition. So only a monopoly’s profit can last.

AThe market demand for pickleball shifts left until P = ATC.No The trap: exit moves each club’s demand, not the market’s.
